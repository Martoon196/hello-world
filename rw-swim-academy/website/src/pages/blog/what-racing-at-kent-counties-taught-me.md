There's a row of plastic chairs behind the blocks at every county meet. You sit in it for twenty minutes before your race, headphones in, not talking to anyone, swimming the race in your head about forty times. Then a whistle, and about a minute in which everything you've practised either shows up or it doesn't.

I've sat in that row at the Kent County Championships more times than I can count now, and at national qualifier meets since. I've had races I'm proud of and at least one I cried about in the car afterwards. **[CONFIRM Ruby is happy with this line]**

None of that is why parents should book with me. But it is where my teaching comes from, and I think that's worth explaining. Here are five things racing taught me that I now teach every seven-year-old in Sevenoaks, usually in their first block.

## 1. Slow is fast

Every seven-year-old wants to go fast, so they thrash. Arms everywhere, no glide, out of breath at ten metres.

I did the same until I was about twelve. Then a coach made me swim a length in as few strokes as I could. Count them. Beat the number next time. The fewer strokes, the longer the glide; the longer the glide, the less effort; the less effort, the further you go. The fastest swimmers on the start line are the calmest ones in the water.

So the first "game" I play with a seven-year-old who can swim a width is: fewest strokes wins. They think it's a game. It's the most important technical lesson in swimming, and they get it ten years earlier than I did.

## 2. Breathing is a skill, not a thing that happens

At county level, the difference between a good 100 metres and a bad one is often what you do with your breath in the last 25. Nobody teaches you that in a group of ten at the leisure centre. They teach you to swim a width holding your breath, and then, years later, somebody has to unteach it.

The rule is simple: breathe out under the water, so that when you turn your head, all you have to do is breathe in. If you're blowing out at the surface, you've got no time left to breathe in, and that's the gasp, and that's why a child can swim a width but not a length.

I teach it in week one. Bubbles, humming, breathe out under, turn and breathe in. It's the single thing that moves more children from "can do a width" to "can do 25 metres" than anything else.

## 3. Pace is something you practise

The race I'm least proud of: I went out far too fast, died at 150 metres, and added time to my best. **[CONFIRM the detail is true and Ruby wants it public]** My coach didn't say "never mind". She asked what I'd learned. The answer was that pace is a skill. You practise it or you don't have it.

A nine-year-old who sets off like a rocket and fades isn't trying hard enough at the end. They're trying too hard at the start. I don't tell them to push. I teach them to count, to feel what an even pace is like, and to finish faster than they started. Same lesson as mine, ten years earlier and without the tears in the car.

## 4. The breaststroke kick has an order

Breaststroke is my main stroke, so I'll be specific about it.

The mistake I see most in eight-to-twelve-year-olds: feet turned out too early and knees too wide. It feels powerful. It's a brake. The water is going the wrong way past the legs.

The kick has three parts, in order:

1. **Heels up.** Heels towards the bottom, knees roughly hip-width, not wide.
2. **Feet out.** Only now do the feet turn out, to catch the water.
3. **Whip and squeeze.** Round and together, finishing with the legs long and squeezed, then a glide.

Get the order right and the same effort produces twice the glide. I demonstrate both versions side by side in lessons and the child can see it immediately. Most club-level breaststroke faults I see are this one, and it's fixable in a fortnight if someone is watching every kick, which is why I do stroke clinics as 1:1s with video.

## 5. Being scared is normal, and it goes away when you touch the bottom

I was scared of the deep end until I was nine. It stopped being scary the day I went down, touched the bottom, and came back up. That's the whole thing. Once you know you come back up, deep water is just deeper water.

At county level you learn a version of the same lesson: the nerves in the marshalling chairs don't go away, you just learn that you survive them. So I don't tell nervous seven-year-olds there's nothing to be scared of. I hold their hand, we go down, we touch the bottom, we come up. Week two or three. Not year four by accident.

## Why this matters for a parent choosing lessons

Most swimming teachers have never raced. That's not a criticism; plenty of them are very good. But there's a difference between teaching from a manual and teaching from having stood on the blocks. When I tell a child that slow is fast, or that breathing out under the water is the whole game, it's not a line from a course. It's the thing that made the difference for me, and I can see it at 25 metres when it's missing.

I'm still training, towards the Olympic pathway, which means I'm still being coached, still counting strokes, still fixing my own turns. I think that keeps me honest about how hard this is.

> **From the pool:** a swimmer in the Stroke group arrived with a beautiful, wide, powerful-looking breaststroke kick that was getting them nowhere. We did heels-up, feet-out, whip, for two lessons. By the third they were gliding past the child next to them using less effort, and they asked if they could "do that in a race one day".
