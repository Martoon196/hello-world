Water Safety for Families in Kent: Rivers, Lakes, the Coast and Holiday Pools

Practical water safety for Kent families from a Sevenoaks swimming teacher: rivers, lakes, the coast, and holiday pools. What to teach your child and what to watch for.

18 January 2027 · by Ruby Waller

I teach children to swim, and I want to say something that might sound odd coming from a swimming teacher: being able to swim in a pool does not make a child safe in a river.

It helps. It helps a lot. But the water in Kent that families actually end up near in the summer, the Medway, the Darent, the lakes and reservoirs, the beaches at Camber and Whitstable and Botany Bay, and the hotel pool on holiday, behaves nothing like a heated lane at the leisure centre. Every summer there are stories in the Kent papers that didn't need to happen.

This is the practical version, for parents, from someone who spends her life in water.

The four things every child should be able to do

Before anything else, these are the skills that matter most for safety, in order. They're what I check first in every taster and what the Splash programme is built around.

  1. Get their feet back down. From a back float or a face-down float, roll and stand. A child who can do this in a pool has a chance in shallow open water.
  2. Float on their back and breathe. Thirty seconds, calm, face up. This is the skill that buys time. In cold water it is the single most important thing.
  3. Go under and come up. On purpose, without panic. Children who have never gone under panic when they do it by accident.
  4. Swim to the side and hold on. Ten metres in any direction, then grab and hold. Not a length. Just enough to reach the edge.

If your child can't yet do these four, that's the priority over stroke technique, and it's what I'd work on first.

Rivers: the Medway, the Darent, the Eden and the Stour

Rivers look calm and aren't. What parents don't see:

  • Cold. Even in July, river water in Kent can be cold enough to cause cold water shock: an involuntary gasp and fast breathing that can last a minute or more. A child who gasps with their face in the water inhales water. The advice from the RNLI and RLSS is to float on your back until the shock passes, then act. [CONFIRM current RNLI "Float to Live" wording and link to it]
  • Current. A river that looks still at the surface can be moving fast underneath, especially near weirs. Weirs are the most dangerous feature on any Kent river. Nobody swims near a weir. Nobody.
  • Depth. Riverbeds drop away without warning. "Paddling" becomes "out of depth" in one step.
  • Getting out. Steep, muddy, overgrown banks. A child who can swim may not be able to climb out.

The rule for our family, and the one I'd suggest: paddling only, at a recognised spot, never near a weir or a lock, adult within arm's reach for under-eights, and nobody goes in to fetch a ball or a dog.

Lakes, reservoirs and quarries

Bewl Water, the reservoirs, the flooded quarries you'll find on a walk: the same cold and depth problems as rivers, plus two more. There's often no way to gauge depth at all, and there's nobody around. Reservoirs frequently have signs prohibiting swimming, and the reason is that they've had incidents. Supervised open-water sessions run by a proper venue, with safety cover, wetsuits and a marked course, are a different thing entirely and a good way for older children to learn open water safely. The coast: Camber, Whitstable, Broadstairs, Botany Bay

Kent's beaches range from gently shelving sand to fast tides over flats and rocky bays with rips. What to teach children before you go:

  • Swim between the red and yellow flags where there's a lifeguarded beach. That's where the lifeguards are watching. No flags, no lifeguards.
  • Rip currents. If they feel pulled out, don't swim against it. Float, wave, shout. Then swim parallel to the shore, not towards it, until the pull stops. Practise saying it.
  • Tides. Sandbanks and flats that are safe at low tide can be cut off in minutes. Check the tide times before you set up. Camber and the north Kent flats are known for this.
  • Inflatables. No inflatables in the sea. Offshore wind takes them out faster than any adult can swim. This is the single most common cause of the "child drifting out" callouts every summer. [CONFIRM with RNLI advice]
  • Cold, again. The Channel and the Thames estuary are cold even in August. Short swims, warm up after.

Holiday pools: the one that surprises parents

More children get into trouble in holiday pools than most parents expect, for one reason: everyone assumes someone else is watching.

  • One adult is on duty, always. Say it out loud: "I've got the pool." Not four adults half-watching from sunloungers. One adult, named, not on their phone, until they hand over.
  • Villa pools have no lifeguard, no shallow end marking, and often no fence. Under-fives near a villa pool need an adult within arm's reach. Not near. Within reach.
  • Different depth, different edge. A child who is confident at the leisure centre may not be able to reach the edge in an unfamiliar pool with a wide overflow lip. Check on day one where they can and can't stand.
  • Armbands and inflatables are not safety equipment. They fall off, deflate, and tip children face down. They also teach a vertical body position that makes swimming harder. If you use a flotation aid, use a proper fitted vest and stay within reach.
  • The pool at dusk. Most villa incidents happen when the day is winding down and nobody thinks anyone's swimming. Cover it, or fence it, or put one adult on it until bed.

What to teach at home, this week

  • The "float first" rule. If anything goes wrong in water: roll onto your back, float, breathe, shout. Practise it in the pool until it's automatic.
  • "Don't go in after them." If a friend is in trouble, don't jump in. Shout for an adult, throw something that floats, lie down and reach with a stick. Children drown trying to rescue other children. Adults do too.
  • The three questions before any swim. Is there someone watching? Can I get out? Do I know how deep it is? If any answer is no, don't get in.
  • 999 and "Coastguard". Older children should know that at the coast you ask for the Coastguard.

From the pool: the safety skills I assess in a taster, back float and feet down, going under and up, swim and hold, are the ones parents least often ask about and the ones I care about most. A child who has them is not "safe" in open water, nothing makes anyone that, but they have a chance, and they have time.

If your child doesn't have the four skills yet

The Splash programme for ages 4 to 6 is built around exactly these four skills before any stroke is taught. For older children, a block of 1:1s can get the safety skills in place quickly, and a free taster will tell you honestly where your child is.
