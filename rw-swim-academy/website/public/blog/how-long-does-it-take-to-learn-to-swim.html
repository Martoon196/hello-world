How Long Does It Take a Child to Learn to Swim? Honest Timelines by Age

How long does it take a child to learn to swim? A Sevenoaks swimming teacher gives honest timelines by age, what speeds it up, and what slows it down.

30 November 2026 · by Ruby Waller

"How long will it take?" is the question every parent asks in the five minutes after a taster, and the honest answer starts with another question: what do you mean by "swim"?

If you mean "happy in the water and safe near it", that's one timeline. If you mean "25 metres without stopping", that's another. If you mean "club-ready with three strokes", that's a third. Here's what I've seen, by age, with the caveats that matter.

First, define "can swim"

I use three plain milestones.

Water confident. Gets in without fuss, puts their face in, floats on their back with support, and can get their feet back down on their own. This is the safety milestone. It's the one that matters most.

Can swim. 25 metres of a recognisable stroke, front crawl or breaststroke, without stopping or touching the bottom, with the face in the water and breathing properly. This is what primary schools are meant to get every child to by the end of Year 6.

Swims well. Three strokes with correct technique, can swim 100 metres, understands pace. This is club entry level.

Every timeline below is "lessons per week", assuming one lesson a week in a small group or 1:1. Double the lessons, and you don't quite halve the time, but you get close.

Ages 3 to 4: confidence first

What's realistic: water confidence. Not stroke.

At three or four, the goal is a child who is happy in the water, will put their face in, can float on their back with a hand under them, and can kick to a teacher. In a small, playful setting, most children get there in 8 to 16 lessons. In a big group where they mostly wait, it can take two years, because they're not really being taught.

What I would not expect: 25 metres. Some four-year-olds do it. Most don't, and pushing for it usually costs confidence.

At RW this is the Splash programme: groups of four, 45 minutes, eight weeks.

Ages 5 to 6: the big year

Five and six is where most children make the jump from confident to swimming, if they're taught well.

A confident five-year-old with one small-group or 1:1 lesson a week typically reaches 10 metres in 8 to 12 lessons and 25 metres in 16 to 30. The wide range is mostly breathing: a child who has learned to breathe out under the water gets there fast; a child who gasps at the surface can be stuck at 10 metres for a year.

A nervous five-year-old adds the confidence phase first: 8 to 16 lessons of Splash-type work, then the timeline above.

Ages 7 to 9: the fastest learners

If a seven- or eight-year-old is confident in the water and gets proper attention, they learn to swim faster than any other age. They understand instructions, they can copy a demonstration, and they're not yet self-conscious.

A confident seven-year-old with weekly small-group or 1:1 lessons: 25 metres of front crawl in 8 to 16 lessons, breaststroke to 25 metres in another 8 to 12, backstroke sooner than either.

This is also the age where a plateau is most often the format, not the child. A seven-year-old who's been in the same badge group for two terms almost always has one specific fault that nobody has had time to fix.

At RW this is the Stroke programme.

Ages 10 to 14: fast, if they'll be taught

Older beginners learn quickly because they're strong and they understand. The obstacle is embarrassment. A ten-year-old who can't swim doesn't want to be in a class with six-year-olds, and I don't blame them.

A 1:1 solves that. A confident ten-year-old beginner with 1:1 lessons can usually reach 25 metres in 6 to 12 lessons. A nervous one takes longer because the fear has had longer to set.

For children who already swim and want to get club-ready, the timeline is different: it's about technique, not distance. Expect 8 to 16 focused sessions to get three strokes to a standard a club would accept, depending on where they start. That's the Sprint programme or a stroke clinic.

What speeds it up

  • Attention per minute. A 1:1 or a group of four gives ten times the correction of a group of ten. This is the biggest single factor and it's not close.
  • Frequency. Twice a week beats once. Five days in a row in the holidays beats both for short bursts, because nothing is forgotten between sessions. That's what a holiday intensive is for.
  • Bath-time homework. Bubbles, humming, goggles in the bath. Ten minutes a night moves a nervous child forward faster than an extra lesson.
  • Goggles that fit. Genuinely. Half of "won't put their face in" is leaking goggles.
  • Consistency. Same slot, same day, same teacher. Children who chop and change take longer.

What slows it down

  • Big groups. Three minutes of attention a week isn't enough for a physical skill.
  • Breathing taught late. If a child learns to swim holding their breath, they'll have to unlearn it. Breathing out under the water should be week one, not week ten.
  • Long gaps. A month off and the first lesson back is remembering, not learning. Eight weeks off and a nervous child can be back to the start.
  • Pressure. A deadline the child knows about ("you've got to do it before the holiday") tends to slow the child it's aimed at.

So, honestly: how long?

For a confident five- to eight-year-old with a weekly small-group or 1:1 lesson: two to three blocks of eight weeks to go from "gets in" to 25 metres. For a nervous child, add a block. For a strong ten-year-old beginner, one to two blocks. For a swimmer going club-ready, one to two blocks of technique work.

Anyone who gives you a firm number without seeing your child in the water is guessing. Which is why every swimmer at RW starts with a written assessment, and why I'll tell you what I actually think at the end of it.

From the pool: a swimmer who arrived able to do a width, head up, holding their breath, went from that to 25 metres front crawl with proper breathing in one eight-week block. The thing that changed was breathing out under the water. It had taken two years in a group of ten to not learn it, and one lesson to fix.
