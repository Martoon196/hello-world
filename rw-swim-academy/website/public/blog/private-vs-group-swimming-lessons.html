Private vs Group Swimming Lessons: Which Is Right for Your Child? (A Sevenoaks Parents' Guide)

Private or group swimming lessons in Sevenoaks? A qualified teacher and competitive swimmer explains who each suits, what it costs, and how to decide.

2 November 2026 · by Ruby Waller

Most parents in Sevenoaks don't choose between private and group lessons. They start with group lessons at the leisure centre because that's what's there, and then, a year or two later, they start wondering why their child is still in the same badge group.

I'm Ruby. I'm 18, I've swum competitively for most of my life, and I've now taught in both formats. This is the honest version of the comparison, including the bits where group lessons win.

What a group lesson actually looks like

A typical council or leisure-centre group: eight to ten children, one teacher on the poolside, 30 minutes. Sometimes there's a second helper in the water. Often there isn't.

Do the maths on the attention. Thirty minutes divided by ten children is three minutes of direct attention each, per week. In practice it's less, because a chunk of the lesson is getting everyone lined up and waiting for a turn.

For some children, that's fine. For others, it's why they've been "nearly there" for two terms.

What a private lesson actually looks like

A 1:1 is 30 minutes with one teacher whose whole job is your child. Every width is watched. Every mistake is corrected the moment it happens, rather than once a term. If your child is scared, the teacher is in the water next to them, not calling from the side.

At RW I also teach semi-private (two siblings or friends, similar level) and small groups of four, one ability level, which sit between the two. More on those below.

When group lessons are the right choice

I'm not going to pretend group lessons are useless. They're right when:

  • Your child is confident and progressing. If they're moving up a badge every term or two and they enjoy it, leave them there. It's affordable and it works.
  • The social side matters more than the technical side. Some children swim better with friends around. Group lessons are a good place to be a child in the water.
  • You're topping up a child who already swims. If they can do 25 metres and the goal is "keep them swimming", groups are the sensible place.
  • Budget is tight. A term of council group lessons in Kent is usually cheaper per lesson than any private option. That's just true. [CONFIRM local group lesson price for comparison; typically £8 to £12 per lesson]

When private lessons are the right choice

Private lessons earn their money in four situations. If two of these sound like your child, it's time.

1. They're nervous. A frightened child in a group of ten spends the lesson waiting for their turn to be frightened. In a 1:1 or a group of four, the teacher can go at the child's pace: one foot in, then two, then sitting, then in. Nobody is lifted, nobody is pushed. That's how confidence actually builds, and it can't be done from the poolside with nine others waiting.

2. They've plateaued. Same badge for two terms. They can swim a width but won't swim a length. Usually there's one specific thing holding them back: breathing out under the water, a kick that's working against them, a head position that sinks the hips. A group teacher may spot it. They won't have the time to fix it. A private teacher fixes it 20 times in one lesson.

3. There's a deadline. School swimming in Year 4 or 5, a holiday in July, the 25 metres that primary schools are meant to get every child to by the end of Year 6. If you need real progress in eight weeks, you need the attention per minute that only small numbers give.

4. They're club-track. If your child is in a club or wants to get in, a group lesson at the leisure centre has nothing left to give them. What they need is stroke technique from someone who has raced, and that's a 1:1 or a stroke clinic.

The honest downsides of private lessons

  • Cost. A 1:1 at RW is £35 as a single, or £31 to £32.50 a lesson in a block. That's three to four times a council group lesson. It should also be three to four times as effective, or something's wrong.
  • Intensity. Thirty minutes of one adult's full attention is tiring for a five-year-old. That's why I keep Splash sessions playful and why I don't do 1:1s longer than 30 minutes for under-eights.
  • No peers. Some children genuinely swim better with a friend beside them. That's what semi-private is for.

The middle option most parents don't know about

Small-group programmes solve the trade-off. At RW, the Progress Programme is a group of no more than four swimmers, all at the same level, 45 minutes a week for eight weeks, same day and time throughout. It costs £160 for the block, which works out at £20 a session: more than a council group, a lot less than a 1:1, and every swimmer still gets eyes on them every width.

There are three levels: Splash (water confidence, 4 to 6), Stroke (technique, 6 to 10) and Sprint (club-ready, 9 to 14). Each one starts with a written assessment, has a progress note at week 4, and ends with a written report and a certificate.

Semi-private (2:1) is the other middle option: two siblings or friends at a similar level, £25 each per lesson. Parents like it because it halves the cost and children like it because they're not alone.

How to decide, in one conversation

Ask yourself three questions:

  1. Has my child moved up a level in the last two terms? (No: they need more attention per minute.)
  2. Does my child get in happily? (No: they need someone in the water beside them.)
  3. Can the teacher tell me, specifically, what my child is working on this term? (No: there's no plan for your child, because with ten children there can't be.)

Two or three "no" answers and the format is the problem, not your child.

From the pool: the swimmer I think of when parents ask this had been in the same group for three terms. In the first ten minutes of a taster it was obvious what was wrong: breathing out at the surface instead of under the water, so every breath was a gasp. One correction, repeated for a week, and the "plateau" was gone. Nobody had had the time to see it.
