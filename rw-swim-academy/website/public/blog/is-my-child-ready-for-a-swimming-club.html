Is Your Child Ready for a Swimming Club? A Checklist for Kent Parents

Thinking about a swimming club in Kent? A county swimmer and teacher gives a plain checklist of what clubs expect, what to ask, and how to prepare your child.

4 January 2027 · by Ruby Waller

I joined a swimming club at eight. I remember the trial: a lane, a coach with a clipboard, and a feeling that I was being watched in a way I hadn't been before. I was. That's what a trial is.

Since then I've swum for Sevenoaks and now Maidstone, been selected for the Kent Development Camp, and raced at county and national qualifier level. [CONFIRM clubs] So I get asked this a lot by parents in Sevenoaks and around: is my child ready for a club, and how do I know?

Here's the honest checklist. It's in three parts: what clubs actually look for, what to ask a club, and how to get your child ready if they're not quite there.

Part 1: What clubs actually look for

Every club is different and this isn't any specific club's policy, but from what I've seen and been told, a coach at a trial is checking for most of the following. Tick honestly.

Distance

  • Can swim 50 metres of front crawl without stopping, with the face in and breathing to the side (not lifting the head).
  • Can swim 25 metres of backstroke in a straight line.
  • Can swim 25 metres of recognisable breaststroke (kick and arms in something like the right order).
  • Can do something that looks like butterfly for a few metres, or at least a dolphin kick. (Not always required for the youngest squads.)

Water skills

  • Comfortable in deep water. Can tread water for 30 seconds.
  • Can push off the wall in a streamline (arms overhead, hands together) and glide.
  • Can do a basic tumble turn, or is willing to learn one without panicking about going upside down.
  • Can dive in from the side (from a crouch is fine at first).

The bits nobody tells you about

  • Can listen to an instruction from the poolside and do it, first time, without needing it repeated.
  • Can swim in a lane with other children without stopping in the middle or holding the rope.
  • Can cope with being tired and keep going for a bit.
  • Wants to. This is the big one. A child who is there because a parent wants it lasts about a term.

If your child ticks most of the first two sections and all of the third, they're ready for a trial. If they tick the third and only some of the first two, they're ready to get ready, and that's what the rest of this post is about.

Part 2: What to ask a club

Clubs in Kent range from small, friendly, once-a-week outfits to serious performance clubs training most days. Neither is better; they're different jobs. Ask:

  1. How many sessions a week for the entry squad, and at what times? Early mornings come sooner than you'd think.
  2. What's the squad structure and how do children move up? You want a clear answer, not "when the coach thinks they're ready".
  3. How many swimmers per lane per coach? Six to eight is normal for young squads. Twelve is a lot.
  4. Do you expect competition, and when? Some clubs race from the first term. Some wait a year.
  5. What does it cost in total? Membership, Swim England registration, kit, gala entries, travel. It adds up. [CONFIRM current Swim England registration fee if you quote it]
  6. Who is the welfare officer and how do I contact them? Every affiliated club has one. If they can't tell you, walk away.
  7. Can my child do a trial session before we commit? The answer should be yes.

Part 3: How to get ready if they're not quite there

The most common gaps I see in children who want to join a club:

Breathing. They can swim 25 metres but they lift their head to breathe, so 50 metres is exhausting. Fix: breathe out under the water, turn the head, breathe in. Two to four weeks of focused work.

Breaststroke kick. Wide, feet turned out too early, no glide. Fix: heels up, feet out, whip, in that order. Two weeks if someone is watching every kick.

Deep water and going under. Fine in the shallow end, tense in the deep. Fix: touch the bottom and come up, with a hand held, until it's boring.

Streamline and push-off. Most leisure-centre lessons never teach it. Fix: one session, then practice every length.

Stamina. Can do 25, not 50. Fix: this is mostly the breathing. Once the breathing is right, distance comes fast.

For all of these, the format matters. A child getting three minutes of attention in a group of ten will not fix a breathing fault before a trial. A 1:1, a small group of four at the same level, or a one-off stroke clinic with video will.

Two honest things

First: a club is not a private lesson. Club coaches are excellent at fitness, racing and pace. They're coaching a lane, not an individual, so a stroke fault that arrives at the club tends to stay. Fix it before you go, or alongside, not instead.

Second: I am not competing with clubs. I swim for one. The best outcome I can imagine for a Sprint swimmer is that they get into a club they love and I see them at a gala. RW is where you fix the technique that gets them in and keeps them moving up.

From the pool: a swimmer came for a stroke clinic three weeks before a club trial. Front crawl was fine for 25 metres and fell apart at 40 because of head-up breathing. We spent the hour on breathing out under the water and one drill to take home. Three weeks later they swam 50 metres comfortably at the trial.
