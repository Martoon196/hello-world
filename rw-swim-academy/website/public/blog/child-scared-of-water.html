My Child Is Scared of Water: 7 Things That Actually Help (From a Competitive Swimmer)

Child scared of water? A competitive swimmer and qualified teacher in Sevenoaks shares 7 things that actually help nervous children, at home and in the pool.

16 November 2026 · by Ruby Waller

I was the child who wouldn't put her face in.

I'd stand at the edge, I'd cry, I'd agree to get in and then not get in. My mum tells the story with more patience than she felt at the time. And then, eventually, one teacher did one thing differently and the whole problem went away in a fortnight.

So when a parent in Sevenoaks messages me and says "my child is scared of water, is there any point booking?", the answer is yes, and here's what I've learned works, both from being that child and from teaching them.

First, what the fear usually is

A child who is "scared of water" is rarely scared of water. They're scared of one or more of:

  • water going up their nose,
  • not being able to breathe,
  • not being able to touch the bottom,
  • not knowing when the scary bit will end,
  • being made to do something before they're ready.

Every one of those is fixable. The last one is the important one, because it's the one adults cause, usually with the best intentions.

1. Stop trying to get them in

The fastest way to get a frightened child into the water is to stop trying to get them into the water.

I don't talk about swimming with a crying child. I ask what they had for breakfast. I ask if they've got a dog, and what it's called. Then I say: "You don't have to get in. Can you put one foot in and tell me if it's warm or cold?"

One foot is a decision they can make. Then it's two feet. Then sitting on the edge. Then, often, they're in without anyone noticing the moment it happened. Nobody was lifted. Nobody was pushed.

At home: same rule. No lifting them into the bath or the pool. Their body, their decision, every time. It feels slower. It's much faster.

2. Teach them to hum

Water up the nose is the single most common reason a child won't put their face in. The fix takes ten seconds to explain.

Humming pushes air out of the nose. If you're humming, water can't come in. Try it yourself in the bath: hum, put your face in, keep humming. Nothing goes up your nose.

Children think this is a trick, and they're right. Do it in the bath with a bowl of water. Hum for three seconds, face in, up. The face-in problem is usually gone within a week.

3. Count, so it ends

"Put your face in" is open-ended. To a nervous child, open-ended means forever.

"Face in for three: one, two, three, up" is a promise. It ends. They can cope with three. Next week they can cope with five. The counting is what makes it safe, and it's why I never say "just try".

4. Sort the goggles out

Half of the "won't put their face in" I see is actually "goggles hurt and leak".

Do the suction test: press the goggles to your child's face without the strap. If they stick for a second, they seal. If they drop straight off, they'll leak, and your child will come up gasping and blame the water.

Then loosen the strap. It should be loose enough to get a finger under. The seal does the work, not the strap. A tight strap is why they've got red rings round their eyes and why they cry before they've even got in.

And buy junior size. An adult pair from the kitchen drawer will never seal on a six-year-old's face.

5. Do the bath-time homework

You can do more for a scared swimmer in the bath than most people think. Three things, ten minutes, any evening:

  • Bubbles. Mouth in the water, hum, blow. Breath control starts here.
  • The cup. Pour water over their head with a cup. They hold the cup. Their pace, their choice. It teaches them water on the face is fine.
  • Goggles on. Ten minutes in the bath with goggles, so the first time they wear them isn't in a cold pool with a stranger.

Three weeks of this and a child walks into their first lesson already halfway there.

6. Get the numbers down

A frightened child in a group of ten spends the lesson waiting for their turn to be frightened, with nine children watching. It's the worst possible setting for building confidence.

Water confidence needs an adult in the water, beside them, at their pace. That's a 1:1, or a very small group. At RW, the Splash programme is groups of four, all at the same stage, for exactly this reason. If you can't do private lessons, ask your group teacher whether they can get in the water with the nervous ones, or whether there's a smaller class.

7. Touch the bottom early, together

The deep end stops being scary the day a child touches the bottom and comes back up. Most children discover this by accident at nine or ten. I do it deliberately in the second or third week, holding their hand: go down, touch, push up. Once they know they come back up, the deep end is just deeper water.

The same goes for going under. Ducking under on purpose, with a count and a hand to hold, is what turns "what if I go under?" into "I can go under".

What not to do

  • Don't say "there's nothing to be scared of". There is, to them, and now you've told them their feelings are wrong.
  • Don't bribe with big rewards. It raises the stakes on something you want to feel small.
  • Don't compare with siblings or friends. "Your brother did it at four" has never once worked.
  • Don't let a bad lesson end the swimming. Take a week off, then go back. A month off makes the next start harder.

From the pool: a swimmer I taught during a taster had "never put their face in" according to their parent. Twelve minutes into the session, humming, they did it. Not because of me. Because nobody had told them about the humming.
